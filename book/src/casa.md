Despite appearances to the contrary, computers are fundamentally social machines.[^1] We ought, then, to regard the *use* of computers as inherently social. **Computing As Social Activity**, or CASA, is a set of principles meant to align the ways we use, design and understand computers with social values that stand in support of communities, rather than work against them.

## Principles

1. Computing is inherently social.
2. Therefore, our use of computers should always account for community.
3. Accounting for community entails concern for other people.
4. Concern puts a person's agency before their utility.

## Discussion

CASA is born out of the recognition that the development of computing has too often been unprincipled or antisocial. It should come as no surprise, then, that computing often detracts from the welfare of our communities.

Here, "community" refers not only to formal communities (such as contributors to development of a piece of software, or members of an organization) but also to the informal communities that gather around some activity held in common, such as use of some particular piece of software, or participation in a social network. It includes geographically local communities, as well as dispersed groups bound by mutual concerns or interests. At its broadest, it expands to encompass humanity at large.

Importantly, these principles are intended not only for software developers, but as a practical framework for anyone who uses computers. They are as applicable to interacting via social media as they are to software design, to home computing as to enterprise deployment.

As such, these principles are deliberately structured as a corrective to approaches that reduce computing to the interaction between individual and machine. This is less a repudiation of earlier frameworks (such as FOSS)[^2] than an attempt to re-establish computing practices in values that necessarily precede technical concerns. Portions of other frameworks may be compatible with CASA; some will require reconsideration. Those that can be harmonized with the principles above are welcome; those that conflict should be abandoned.

## In practice

Principles must be put into action if they are to serve any purpose. We inherit industries, practices and social situations that were crafted to serve ulterior ends. Unless we are diligent about translating principle into practice, we will tend to reproduce those patterns. Critical re-examination is a necessary first step.

> Always&hellip;
* question the received culture of computing.
* think concertedly about how to align your own behavior with CASA principles.
* challenge any inherited norms that contradict these principles.

> Avoid&hellip;
* uncritically following trends or established practices.
* accepting imperatives that conflict with CASA principles.
* aligning yourself with antithetical projects or groups.

CASA always concerns itself with the agency of others. License to alter code provides high order agency to those with the requisite skills, but is insufficient on its own.

> Always&hellip;
* design and build for agency.
* use accessibility practices in pursuit of agency for everyone.
* seek consent before acting on someone else's behalf.

> Avoid&hellip;
* reducing others to the value that can be extracted from them.
* using dark or hostile patterns to obtain the appearance of consent.
* treating technical competence as the standard for inclusion.

If we hope to make good on the technology's promise as the vehicle and occasion for human connection, care must be taken in how we approach the social functions affected by computing.

> Always&hellip;
* recognize that working toward a common end puts you in community with others.
* work toward establishing just and healthy social relations.
* seek to preserve properly functioning social relations where they already exist.

> Avoid&hellip;
* automating humans out of the social aspects of computing.
* replacing or displacing social activities with computation.
* organizing for control rather than collaboration.

Computing is never free. Someone always pays, whether by financing development, contributing their time and effort, paying retail or subscription prices, bearing the burden of negative externalities, or some other route. Concern for others includes consideration for how those costs are paid and by whom. 

> Always&hellip;
* financially and materially support the communities in which you participate.
* ensure that software and service providers do not bear the costs alone.
* provide proper attribution whenever possible.[^3]

> Avoid&hellip;
* externalizing costs onto other groups or individuals.
* contributing to dangerous concentrations of power and resources.
* exacerbating relations of inequality.

These practices are derived from our initial principles, and so need not be memorized. Knowing the principles allows us to derive them at need. Nor is this list exhaustive. Practical applications will reveal themselves the more we work and play within the CASA framework. In cases where the course of action is unclear, return to the principles for guidance.

[^1]: Computers are made possible by centuries of intellectual exchange. They're constructed by means of direct and indirect collaboration. Their interoperability is due to socially negotiated agreements over protocols and languages. They contribute to a vast, shared infrastructure. Even the activities encompassed by "personal" computing are possible only as the fruits of community. The lone genius capable of computing in isolation from others may be conceivable, but is of little concern to us.

[^2]: The positive rights enumerated by the establishing documents of FLOSS are almost uniformly expressed as individual rights. (Goldsmith, Daniel; [The Original Sin of Free Software](https://lipu.dgold.eu/original-sin)\) Their elaboration into collective rights is limited almost exclusively to what can be accomplished by treating corporations as analogous to persons.

[^3]: Attribution is not solely about giving credit where credit is due. It facilitates the sort of openness that allows members of a community to understand decisions by asking the person who made a decision. It also makes accountability possible.
